# The consulting canon, by question

Choose the method by the question, not by habit. Name the source of every method you use. Apply it to the product's own data and write the analysis in your own words. Quote at most one short line, with attribution. For the newest findings, benchmarks and dated facts, read `om-knowledge-canon-marketing`, `om-knowledge-canon-sales`, `om-knowledge-canon-brand`, `om-knowledge-management-canon` and `om-knowledge-consulting`; each is grouped by firm (McKinsey, BCG, Bain, Deloitte, PwC, EY, KPMG, Accenture, HBR, HBS). They are verified against the firms' and journals' own pages. This file holds the evergreen methods.

## Framing and solving the problem
- **Problem statement, then an issue tree** (McKinsey practice; Conn & McLean, *Bulletproof Problem Solving*, 2018). Define the decision, success criteria, scope, constraints and decision makers. Split the question into branches that are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive (MECE). Prioritize by impact and by your ability to change it. Work the top branches first.
- **Hypothesis-driven work.** State the likely answer on day one. List the analyses that would prove or kill it, and run only those. Use 80/20 everywhere.
- **Pyramid principle and SCQ** (Barbara Minto, *The Pyramid Principle*). Put the answer first, then the supporting arguments, then the evidence. Open with situation, complication and question. See `om-exec-storyline`.

## Where to compete and how to win
- **Five forces** (Michael Porter, HBR 1979; updated in HBR 2008). Rivalry, new entrants, substitutes, buyer power and supplier power together explain the price and margin an industry allows.
- **What strategy is** (Porter, "What Is Strategy?", HBR 1996). Operational effectiveness is not strategy. Strategy means a unique position, trade-offs (what you will not do) and fit among activities.
- **Value chain and generic strategies** (Porter, 1980 and 1985). Win on cost, on differentiation, or by focusing on a niche. Map where the product's activities create or lose value.
- **Strategy as integrated choices** (Lafley & Martin, *Playing to Win*, HBR Press, 2013). Set the winning aspiration, where to play, how to win, the capabilities and the management systems. Each choice must reinforce the others.
- **Blue ocean** (Kim & Mauborgne, HBR 2004; book 2005). Use the strategy canvas and the eliminate, reduce, raise and create grid to escape head-to-head competition.
- **Coherence and capabilities-driven strategy** (Strategy& of PwC; Leinwand & Mainardi, HBR 2010 and *Strategy That Works*, 2016). Choose a way to play, build a small system of distinctive capabilities, and align the product and service portfolio to it.

## Where growth comes from
- **Growth decomposition and granularity** (McKinsey, *The Granularity of Growth*, 2007). Split growth into market momentum, M&A and share gain, at the finest grain the data allows (segment × channel × market). Averages hide where the growth is.
- **Three horizons** (McKinsey; Baghai, Coley & White, *The Alchemy of Growth*, 1999). H1 defends and extends the core, H2 builds emerging businesses, H3 creates options. Fund all three on purpose.
- **Ansoff matrix** (Igor Ansoff, HBR 1957). Choose between market penetration, market development, product development and diversification. Risk rises as you move away from the core.
- **Portfolio matrices.** The BCG growth-share matrix (Bruce Henderson, BCG, 1970) uses market growth and relative share. The GE–McKinsey nine-box uses market attractiveness and competitive strength. Use them to allocate effort across products, segments or markets, not as labels.
- **Experience curve** (BCG, 1960s). Unit costs fall predictably with cumulative volume. That is why share can drive cost advantage, and why price-led share grabs need the cost curve behind them.
- **Founder's mentality** (Bain; Zook & Allen, 2016). An insurgent mission, a frontline obsession and an owner mindset. Growth stalls when scale brings bureaucracy.

## Customers and value
- **Marketing myopia** (Theodore Levitt, HBR 1960). Define the business by the customer need it serves, not by the product.
- **Jobs to be done** (Clayton Christensen et al., HBS; HBR 2016). Customers hire a product to make progress in a circumstance. Design for the job, the circumstance and the forces that push buyers toward a switch or hold them back.
- **Elements of value** (Bain; Almquist, Senior & Bloch, HBR 2016: 30 consumer elements in a pyramid; HBR 2018: 40 B2B elements). Score the product and its rivals on the elements buyers care about. Products that deliver more elements tend to earn more loyalty.
- **Consumer decision journey** (McKinsey Quarterly, 2009). Buyers consider, evaluate, buy, experience and advocate, and loyal buyers loop back without re-shopping. Find the stage where the product loses people.
- **Net Promoter System** (Fred Reichheld, Bain; HBR 2003). Promoters minus detractors. The value is in the closed loop: following up with customers and fixing root causes. Always pair NPS with behaviour data.
- **Loyalty economics** (Reichheld & Sasser, "Zero Defections", HBR 1990) and the **service-profit chain** (Heskett et al., HBS; HBR 1994). Retention compounds profit, and employee and service quality drive customer loyalty.
- **Customer experience pillars** (KPMG Customer Experience Excellence Centre). Personalization, integrity, expectations, resolution, time and effort, and empathy. Score each key journey on all six.

## Price and the commercial engine
- **Pocket-price waterfall** (Marn & Rosiello, McKinsey; HBR 1992). Walk from list price to the price actually banked, through every discount, rebate and allowance. Leakage is usually the fastest profit lever.
- **Value-based pricing.** Price to the value delivered to each segment. Test willingness to pay before building (see `om-knowledge-pricing`: Van Westendorp, conjoint and Gabor-Granger).
- **Sales effectiveness.** Segment accounts by potential and share, set coverage by segment, raise the share of seller time spent selling, and enforce discount rules through a deal desk.

## Innovation
- **Disruptive innovation** (Bower & Christensen, HBR 1995; Christensen, *The Innovator's Dilemma*, 1997; "What Is Disruptive Innovation?", HBR 2015). Entrants win from the low end or from non-consumers with simpler, cheaper offers that incumbents ignore. Disruption is a process, not a label for any new idea.
- **Ten types of innovation** (Doblin, part of Deloitte; Keeley et al., 2013). The ten types are profit model, network, structure, process, product performance, product system, service, channel, brand and customer engagement. Combining several types beats product features alone.
- **Lean start-up** (Steve Blank, HBR 2013). Run hypotheses, a minimum viable product and customer development before scaling.

## Organization, decisions and change
- **7-S** (McKinsey: Peters, Waterman, Athos & Pascale, around 1980). Strategy, structure and systems (hard) must fit shared values, style, staff and skills (soft).
- **Decision rights: RAPID** (Rogers & Blenko, Bain; "Who Has the D?", HBR 2006). For each key decision, name who recommends, who must agree, who gives input, who decides and who performs it.
- **Leading change** (John Kotter, HBS; HBR 1995). Urgency, a guiding coalition, vision, communication, removing obstacles, short-term wins, consolidating, anchoring in culture.
- **Influence model** (McKinsey Quarterly, 2003). Change behaviour through role modelling, understanding and conviction, talent and skills, and reinforcing mechanisms, all four together.
- **Zero-based budgeting** (Peter Pyhrr, HBR 1970). Rebuild every cost line from zero against the outcome it buys. Then reinvest the savings in growth.

## Measurement and execution
- **Balanced scorecard and strategy maps** (Kaplan & Norton, HBS; HBR 1992 and 2000). Link financial, customer, process and learning objectives by cause and effect. Each objective gets a measure, a target and an initiative.
- **Marketing effectiveness.** Triangulate the marketing mix model, incrementality experiments and attribution. Balance brand building and activation (see `om-knowledge-consulting` and `om-knowledge-research` for the evidence and dated benchmarks).

## Decisions under uncertainty
- **Scenario planning** (Pierre Wack, Shell; HBR 1985). Build plausible futures from the critical uncertainties. Then find the no-regret moves, the options worth buying now and the signposts to watch.
- **Pre-mortem** (Gary Klein, HBR 2007). Imagine the plan has failed and list why, before committing to it.
- **Case method** (Harvard Business School). Frame each decision around a protagonist, a deadline, real options, criteria, the evidence on both sides and a structured debate. Then the owner decides.

## Quality bar (what a partner checks)
1. One governing question and one answer, stated in the first lines.
2. Every page title is a full-sentence finding, and the titles alone tell the story.
3. Every number has a source and a date. Estimates are labelled, with their assumptions.
4. Options are compared on stated criteria, including "do nothing".
5. Every recommendation has an owner, a metric, a first step and a stop rule.
6. It uses only claims from `claims.md`. Nothing goes out without approval. No budget cap changes.
