## BCG

- **BCG growth-share matrix (Bruce Henderson, BCG 1970): plot products by relative market share and market growth. Stars (high share, high growth) may not fund themselves; cash cows (high share, low growth) throw off surplus cash that should not be reinvested in the same product; question marks (low share, high growth) need cash and are liabilities unless they become leaders; pets (low share, low growth) are near liquidation value. Use for portfolio cash allocation across products; it ignores synergies and assumes share drives margin.** (1970-01-01; primary, high) [source](https://www.bcg.com/publications/1970/strategy-the-product-portfolio) `K-9296a64d32`

## Harvard Business Review

- **Porter's five forces (HBR, 2008 update of the 1979 article): industry profitability is shaped by rivalry among established competitors, the bargaining power of customers and of suppliers, the threat of new entrants and the threat of substitutes. Managers often define competition too narrowly as today's direct rivals. Use at market entry or category choice to find a position that is more profitable and less exposed; it describes industry structure, not how to win inside it.** (2008-01-01; research, high) [source](https://hbr.org/2008/01/the-five-competitive-forces-that-shape-strategy) `K-97d41c0904`
- **Porter, 'What Is Strategy?' (HBR 1996): operational effectiveness (doing the same activities better) is necessary but rivals copy it, so it rarely yields lasting advantage. Strategy means performing different activities or the same ones differently, choosing trade-offs (what not to do) and building fit so activities reinforce each other. Use to test a plan: if a competitor could copy it by adopting best practice, it is not a strategy. Not a template for tactics.** (1996-11-01; research, high) [source](https://hbr.org/1996/11/what-is-strategy) `K-1fea3fa7da`

- **Gabor-Granger pricing: show a respondent a (randomized) price and ask purchase likelihood, then step prices up or down until intent changes. Output a demand curve, a revenue curve (price x share willing to buy) and its revenue-maximizing price (profit-maximizing if costs are known). Use for new launches or repricing one product. Limits: no competitor context and no attribute trade-offs; use conjoint for those, and Van Westendorp for a price range.** (2024-03-12; primary, high) [source](https://sawtoothsoftware.com/resources/blog/posts/gabor-granger-pricing-method) `K-4f18819588`
- **Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter: ask four questions (too cheap to trust quality, a bargain, getting expensive but still considered, too expensive) and plot cumulative curves; crossings give the optimal and indifference price points and the acceptable range between marginal cheapness and marginal expensiveness. Sawtooth advises using it mainly for new-to-the-world products and adding Newton-Miller-Smith purchase-intent questions; it ignores competitors and does not predict sales on its own.** (2024-03-05; primary, high) [source](https://www.sawtoothsoftware.com/resources/blog/posts/van-westendorp-pricing-sensitivity-meter) `K-aeaae586ad`

- **Marketing mix modeling data rules (Meta Robyn analyst's guide): use at least two years of weekly data (4-5 years if only monthly), actual spend and impressions, digital split at least by channel, and about 10 observations per independent variable (n about 7-10x p). Use to decide if a client is ready for MMM; small or new brands usually are not.** (2023-01-01; official, high) [source](https://facebookexperimental.github.io/Robyn/docs/analysts-guide-to-MMM/) `K-592d13f080`

- **Mental availability metrics from a CEP survey (Romaniuk): Mental Market Share = brand's CEP links / all brands' CEP links; Mental Penetration = share of category buyers linking the brand to at least one CEP; Network Size = average links among those with mental penetration. - **Category entry points (CEPs, Jenni Romaniuk): the buying situations and cues that bring a category to mind, phrased as 'looking for a brand that...'. Then prioritise CEPs, build them into communications and measure linkage. Use to pick which situations ads should link the brand to. Not a list of channels or places to buy.** (2022-06-01; research, medium) [source](https://business.linkedin.com/content/dam/lem/business/en/advertise/resources/b2b-institute/b2bi-cepinb2b-final.pdf) `K-6ad29f2534`
- **Positioning in five components (April Dunford): competitive alternatives (what customers would use if you did not exist, including doing nothing), differentiated capabilities, the value those deliver, the target customers who care most about that value, and the market category that makes the value obvious. Not a messaging or tagline exercise; messaging comes after.** (2021-03-15; primary, high) [source](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/a-quickstart-guide-to-positioning) `K-c2f714ca3e`
- **95:5 rule (John Dawes, Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, as reported by Marketing Week): only about 5% of B2B buyers are in market to buy at any given time; the other 95% will not buy for months or even years. Implication: judge B2B marketing on reach of all category buyers and brand building, not only short-term leads. Use when a B2B plan is all bottom-funnel. A rule of thumb from an industry research programme (not peer-reviewed), not a precise ratio; the share in market varies with each category's purchase cycle. The page is an undated news repost.** (2021-01-01; research, medium) [source](https://marketingscience.info/news-and-insights/the-955-rule-is-the-new-6040-rule) `K-80f38d0a21`
- **Brand/activation balance (Binet & Field, IPA Databank): in B2C, efficiency is typically maximised with about 60% of the communications budget on brand building and 40% on activation; in B2B the optimum is about 46% brand / 54% activation (1998-2018 B2B cases). Use as a starting split for an established brand with enough budget for both, then adjust by category, price point and brand size. Not a law: the B2B estimate rests on fewer than 50 award-entry cases skewed to the UK and large budgets.** (2019-01-01; primary, medium) [source](https://business.linkedin.com/content/dam/lem/business/en/advertise/resources/b2b-institute/b2b-research/LIN_B2B-Marketing-Report-Digital-v02.pdf) `K-05595ca415`
- **In B2B, customer acquisition (penetration) strategies tend to be much more effective at driving growth than customer retention/loyalty strategies, matching B2C evidence (Binet & Field, B2B IPA Databank cases 1998-2018, fewer than 50 cases). A LinkedIn survey cited in the report suggests many B2B marketers nonetheless place more belief in loyalty-based strategies. Use to weight plans toward reaching new category buyers rather than only upselling existing accounts. Not an argument to neglect retention economics in subscription businesses; it concerns what drives share growth.** (2019-01-01; primary, medium) [source](https://business.linkedin.com/content/dam/lem/business/en/advertise/resources/b2b-institute/b2b-research/LIN_B2B-Marketing-Report-Digital-v02.pdf) `K-059afb9b82`
- **Excess share of voice (ESOV = share of voice minus share of market): brands that keep SOV above SOM tend to grow, those below tend to shrink. In the IPA Databank, 10 points of ESOV are associated with about +0.6 points of market share a year for consumer brands and +0.7 for B2B, all else equal (Binet & Field). Use to set budgets: pick a share target, set the SOV needed, then estimate the spend. Not for brand-new categories or when SOV cannot be measured; B2B results are tentative (small sample).** (2019-01-01; primary, medium) [source](https://business.linkedin.com/content/dam/lem/business/en/advertise/resources/b2b-institute/b2b-research/LIN_B2B-Marketing-Report-Digital-v02.pdf) `K-0ce5f18bb6`
- **Campaign objective matters (Binet & Field, IPA Databank B2B cases 1998-2018): campaigns with the objective of building brand fame deliver far more large business effects than those just building awareness. In B2B, emotional messaging is better at building brands and driving long-term sales and profit, while rational arguments and product detail work best in activation campaigns aimed at buyers most likely to buy. Use to set brand-campaign objectives and pair emotional brand work with rational activation. Small sample (fewer than 50 B2B cases) from award entries, so treat as directional.** (2019-01-01; primary, medium) [source](https://business.linkedin.com/content/dam/lem/business/en/advertise/resources/b2b-institute/b2b-research/LIN_B2B-Marketing-Report-Digital-v02.pdf) `K-3f7cd5e1cc`
- **Peter Field's 'The Crisis in Creative Effectiveness' (IPA, ~600 IPA Effectiveness Award cases, 1996-2018): creatively awarded campaigns had become no more effective than non-awarded ones, the lowest point in 24 years of data, with short-termism (more short-term sales activation among awarded work) named as the driver. Use when advising that creative awards are not proof of business effect and that creative should serve long-term brand goals. Award-entry data, UK-skewed.** (2019-01-01; research, high) [source](https://ipa.co.uk/knowledge/publications-reports/the-crisis-in-creative-effectiveness) `K-4d6a24917b`
- **OKRs (Objectives and Key Results), created by Andy Grove at Intel and brought to Google by John Doerr in 1999: an Objective states what to achieve and typically 3-5 Key Results measure progress. Separate committed OKRs (expected to be fully met) from aspirational stretch OKRs. Grade at cycle end (yes/no, red-yellow-green, or Google's 0.0-1.0). Use to tie marketing work to a revenue objective each quarter; not a task list.** (2018-01-01; primary, high) [source](https://www.whatmatters.com/faqs/okr-meaning-definition-example) `K-34d3054d3b`
- **Creative quality multiplies media weight (System1, from Peter Field's IPA analysis): an average 2-star ad delivers about 0.5 points of market share gain per 10 points of ESOV, while a 5-star ad delivers about 3 points; only 4% of the many thousands of ads System1 tested reach 5 stars. Use to argue that pretesting and improving creative can be worth more than extra spend. Vendor-published figures from System1's own rating; no sample size or method detail on the page.** (2017-11-03; primary, medium) [source](https://system1group.com/blog/the-system1-guarantee-how-what-why) `K-f90f11bced`
- **Jobs to be done (Christensen Institute): a job is the progress a person wants to make in a particular circumstance; people pull products into their lives to make that progress. Jobs have functional, social and emotional dimensions. In the milkshake case, morning buyers used a shake to stave off hunger and ease the commute, so the real competition was bananas and bagels, not other shakes. Use to define competitors by the alternatives buyers actually consider and to find switching triggers; not a substitute for demand sizing.** (2016-01-01; research, high) [source](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/theory/jobs-to-be-done/) `K-0da4a78825`
- **Marketing's impact on firm value (Edeling & Fischer, JMR meta-analysis of 488 elasticities from 83 studies): mean stock-market elasticity is 0.04 for advertising spend and 0.54 for marketing assets; customer-related assets (0.72) beat brand-related assets (0.33). Advertising elasticities are lower in concentrated industries, and asset elasticities are higher in recessions. Use when making the finance case: build customer and brand assets, not just spend. Elasticities vary with study design.** (2015-11-05; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.14.0046) `K-48df4bb2bd`
- **Spending in recessions (Srinivasan, Lilien & Sridhar, Journal of Marketing 2011; 10,000+ firm-years of US public firms, 1969-2008, seven recessions): whether raising R&D or advertising in a recession helps profits and stock returns depends on market share, financial leverage and B2B/B2C goods or services. Examples: 98% of B2C goods firms underspend on R&D and 92% of B2C services firms overspend on advertising. Use for downturn budget advice, case by case.** (2011-04-15; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkg.75.3.49) `K-f35ee0fef3`
- **Geo experiments (Vaver & Koehler, Google 2011): randomly assign non-overlapping geographic regions to treatment or control, run geo-targeted ads only in treatment, and compare outcomes to estimate ad effectiveness. Google reports the method is conceptually simple, has a systematic design process and gives easy-to-read results, including for search spend. Use when user-level holdouts are impossible (privacy limits, offline sales). Needs enough comparable regions and volume.** (2011-01-01; primary, high) [source](https://research.google/pubs/measuring-ad-effectiveness-using-geo-experiments/) `K-cc4304478e`
- **Net Promoter is not uniquely predictive (Keiningham et al., Journal of Marketing 2007): using longitudinal data from 21 firms and 15,500+ interviews in the Norwegian Customer Satisfaction Barometer, plus ACSI comparisons, the authors could not replicate claims that Net Promoter is clearly superior to other measures for predicting revenue growth in the industries cited as exemplars. Use NPS as one diagnostic, not the single growth KPI; pair it with retention and revenue metrics.** (2007-07-01; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkg.71.3.039) `K-bd0b3e5fa6`
- **Forecasting repeat purchases for LTV (Fader, Hardie & Lee, Marketing Science 2005): the BG/NBD model predicts future transactions from customers' past purchasing, gives results very similar to the Pareto/NBD 'counting your customers' model in a wide range of settings, and its parameters can be estimated in Excel. Use for non-contractual businesses (e-commerce, marketplaces) where churn is unobserved; for subscriptions, model observed churn directly.** (2005-05-01; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.1040.0098) `K-77a876f2cb`
- **Brand salience / mental availability (Romaniuk & Sharp, Marketing Theory 2004): salience is the brand's propensity to be noticed or come to mind in buying situations, reflecting the quantity and quality of memory links buyers hold. It is broader than 'top of mind' first mention and distinct from awareness or attitude. Use to brief brand tracking on links to buying situations, not just unaided awareness. Not a measure of liking or preference.** (2004-12-01; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1177/1470593104047643) `K-d285e02d4d`
- **Valuing customers (Gupta, Lehmann & Stuart, JMR 2004): customer value is the expected sum of discounted future earnings. Across five public firms, a 1% improvement in retention raised firm value by about 5%, versus 1% for a 1% margin improvement and 0.1% for a 1% cut in acquisition cost; a 1% retention gain had almost five times the impact of a 1% change in discount rate. Use to prioritise retention work in subscription models; based on five firms, so a direction, not a constant.** (2004-01-14; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.41.1.7.25084) `K-e116543e3e`
- **Return on marketing via customer equity (Rust, Lemon & Zeithaml, Journal of Marketing 2004): trade off strategy options by projected change in customer equity (sum of current and future customers' lifetime values) relative to the incremental spend. Lifetime value comes from category purchase frequency, quantity, brand-switching patterns and contribution margin; switching can be estimated with a logit model on panel or survey data. Use to compare investments (quality, ads, loyalty) on one financial scale.** (2004-01-01; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkg.68.1.109.24030) `K-b6becb5b2d`
- **Customer-based brand equity (Keller, Journal of Marketing 1993): brand equity is the differential effect of brand knowledge on consumer response to the brand's marketing, compared with the same marketing for an unnamed or fictitious version. Brand knowledge = brand awareness plus brand image (associations); equity exists when the consumer is familiar with the brand and holds favorable, strong and unique associations. Use to structure brand audits and tracking; not a financial valuation method.** (1993-01-01; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1177/002224299305700101) `K-f29639ae96`
- **Conjoint analysis (Green & Srinivasan, Journal of Marketing 1990, updating their 1978 review): estimate how buyers trade off product attributes, including price, from their preferences across attribute combinations; the review covers approaches for many attributes, reliability and validity checks, and choice simulators that predict share for new configurations. Use for packaging, tier design and price within a competitive set. Needs careful attribute design and an adequate sample; heavier than Van Westendorp or Gabor-Granger.** (1990-10-01; research, medium) [source](https://doi.org/10.1177/002224299005400402) `K-920278b553`
- **Double jeopardy (Ehrenberg, Goodhardt & Barwise, Journal of Marketing 1990): in any period a small brand has far fewer buyers than a large brand, and its buyers also buy it somewhat less often. Loyalty differences between brands mostly follow size. Use to set realistic goals: a brand grows mainly by adding buyers (penetration), so plans that promise share gains from loyalty alone are suspect. Known exceptions exist (niche and change-of-pace brands).** (1990-07-01; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1177/002224299005400307) `K-6760d0a1cd`
- **Bass diffusion model (Management Science 1969): the timing of first purchase of a new product depends on innovative and imitative behavior, with imitation tied to the number of previous buyers; tested on 11 consumer durables, it predicted the sales peak and its timing well. Use to forecast adoption curves for a new category or product once a few periods of sales exist, or by analogy to similar products. Not for repeat-purchase sales or for brands within an established category.** (1969-01-01; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.15.5.215) `K-3620695c48`
