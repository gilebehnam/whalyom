## Research findings

- **TV ad ROI at scale (Shapiro, Hitsch & Tuchman, Econometrica 2021, 288 brands): TV advertising elasticities are substantially smaller than published estimates, with many insignificant or negative. Marginal ROI is negative for more than 80% of brands, implying over-investment, and the overall ROI of observed ad schedules is positive for only about a third of brands. Results are robust to model form and not driven by low power. Use to challenge TV budgets without lift evidence; US packaged goods context.** (2021-01-01; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.3982/ecta17674) `K-09a80e0de6`
- **Observational ad measurement vs experiments (Gordon, Zettelmeyer, Bhargava & Chapsky, Marketing Science 2019): across 15 US Facebook ad experiments (about 500 million user-experiment observations, 1.6 billion impressions), observational methods often failed to reproduce the randomized lift, even with rich demographic and behavioral controls and modern causal-inference methods. Use to require randomized lift tests before trusting attribution-based ROAS.** (2019-03-01; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2018.1135) `K-37c66a1282`
- **Email personalization field experiments (Sahni, Wheeler & Chintagunta, Marketing Science 2018, millions of recipients, three companies): adding the recipient's name to the subject line raised open rate by 20% (9.05% to 10.80%), sales leads by 31% (0.39% to 0.51%) and cut unsubscribes by 17% (1.2% to 1.0%). Effects held for acquisition and retention emails. Use name personalization as a default test; it seems to work by raising attention to the rest of the message, not by adding information.** (2018-02-27; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2017.1066) `K-02e4697c1b`
- **Brand keyword defense (Simonov, Nosko & Rao, Marketing Science 2018, Bing experiments, thousands of brands): with no competitors bidding, brand ads add only 1-4% clicks (less for big brands), so cost per incremental click is high. Competitors in paid positions 2-4 take 1-5% of clicks when the brand advertises, but 18-42% when the brand does not, making defensive brand bidding strongly ROI-positive when rivals bid. Use to decide brand-term bidding by checking competitor presence first.** (2018-01-29; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.2017.1065) `K-ee56ff80f0`
- **Ghost ads (Johnson, Lewis & Nubbemeyer, JMR 2017): identifying the control-group users who would have seen an ad gives cleaner, cheaper lift tests than PSA or intent-to-treat designs and works with real-time optimizing ad platforms. In an online retailer's display retargeting campaign, ads lifted site visits by 17.2% and purchases by 10.5%, measured at least an order of magnitude more cheaply. Use when choosing lift-test designs; the lift figures are from one retailer.** (2017-03-07; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.15.0297) `K-f07b4961ad`
- **Creative devices and sales (Hartnett et al., Journal of Advertising, 312 TV ads, several categories and countries): replicating Stewart and Furse's codebook of 150+ creative devices against in-market short-term sales, no single creative device did much alone to explain sales effectiveness, and the most and least effective devices differed from the original copy-test study. Use to avoid creative checklists as guarantees; test executions in market.** (2015-09-29; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1080/00913367.2015.1077491) `K-b424390596`
- **eWOM and sales meta-analysis (Babic Rosario et al., JMR 2016; 1,532 effect sizes, 96 studies, 40 platforms, 26 categories): eWOM correlates positively with sales (average r = .091). Volume matters more than valence; negative eWOM does not always hurt sales, but high rating variability does. On social platforms, effects are stronger when receivers can judge similarity to senders. Use to aim review programmes at more reviews and consistent experience, not only a higher average.** (2015-08-17; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.14.0380) `K-4b24f6fa5c`
- **Measuring ad returns is noisy (Lewis & Rao, QJE 2015): 25 large field experiments with US retailers and brokerages ($2.8M of digital ads, most reaching millions) had a median confidence interval on ROI over 100 percentage points wide; individual sales are so volatile (coefficient of variation around 10) that informative tests can need over 10 million person-weeks. Selection bias cripples observational methods. Use to set honest test sizes and to avoid over-reading small lift tests.** (2015-07-06; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjv023) `K-e5abdd8ec1`
- **Ad repetition meta-analysis (Schmidt & Eisend, Journal of Advertising 2015): in experimental settings, brand attitude peaks at about ten exposures, while recall rises linearly and does not level off before the eighth exposure. Spaced exposures and low involvement strengthen attitude effects; repetition effects decay over time. Use to set frequency caps and flighting (space exposures rather than bunching). Lab exposures are forced, so real-world in-feed needs may differ.** (2015-03-25; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1080/00913367.2015.1018460) `K-afa292789f`
- **eWOM elasticity meta-analysis (You, Vadakkepatt & Joshi, Journal of Marketing 2015; 51 studies, 339 volume and 271 valence elasticities): sales elasticity to review volume is 0.236 and to valence (rating) 0.417. Both are higher for privately consumed, low-trialability products in less competitive industries and for reviews on independent review sites. Use to estimate the payoff of review programmes; strongest for products buyers cannot try first.** (2015-01-14; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1509/jm.14.0169) `K-c29e39583b`
- **Paid search incrementality at eBay (Blake, Nosko & Tadelis, Econometrica 2015): large field experiments showed returns from paid search are a fraction of non-experimental estimates. Brand-keyword ads had no measurable short-term benefit; non-brand ads positively influenced new and infrequent users, but frequent users, who would buy anyway, absorbed most spend, so average returns were negative. Use to test brand-term and loyal-user spend with holdouts; effects differ when competitors bid on your brand.** (2015-01-01; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.3982/ecta12423) `K-b87bf880c9`
- **When choice overload does occur (Chernev, Bockenholt & Goodman, JCP 2015; 99 observations, N=7,202): large assortments hurt choice when choice sets are complex, the decision task is difficult, preferences are uncertain, or buyers want to minimize effort; with these moderators accounted for, the assortment effect is significant. Use to simplify pricing pages and plan grids for novice or low-effort buyers, and to add recommendations or defaults when many options are needed.** (2014-08-28; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002) `K-a1cbfdd82d`
- **Experimentation at scale (Kohavi et al., KDD 2013): Microsoft Bing ran over 200 concurrent controlled experiments a day; many ideas move key metrics by about 1% (worth over $10M a year in US revenue) yet are not well estimated in advance, and experiments caught many negative features stakeholders were excited about. Trustworthiness issues include frequent false positives and interactions between experiments. Use to justify testing over opinion and to guard against false positives.** (2013-08-11; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1145/2487575.2488217) `K-18f008d8f1`
- **Dynamic retargeting (Lambrecht & Tucker, JMR 2013, online travel field experiment): ads showing the exact products a user viewed were on average less effective than generic brand ads. They stopped underperforming once browsing showed refined preferences (e.g., visiting review sites). Use to show generic or category creative to early-stage visitors and product-specific creative only to users deep in research. One travel firm, so test in your category.** (2013-07-09; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmr.11.0503) `K-b9b7e6bbb1`
- **Advertising elasticity meta-analysis (Sethuraman, Tellis & Briesch, JMR 2011): 751 short-term and 402 long-term brand advertising elasticities from 56 studies (1960-2008). Mean short-term elasticity is 0.12 (a 10% ad increase gives about 1.2% more sales) and long-term 0.24, both below earlier estimates, and elasticity has declined over time. It is higher for durables, early life-cycle brands, and GRP-based measures. Use for realistic ad-response assumptions; brand-level averages, not channel-specific.** (2011-05-12; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.48.3.457) `K-88c7a9e288`
- **Targeting vs obtrusiveness (Goldfarb & Tucker, Marketing Science 2011, large field experiment): matching a display ad to page content and making an ad more obtrusive each raised purchase intent, but combining both performed worse than either alone. The penalty was strongest for privacy-sensitive people and categories. Use to avoid pairing tight contextual targeting with intrusive formats, especially in sensitive categories like health or finance.** (2011-02-09; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.1100.0583) `K-10f9e08722`
- **Personal selling elasticity meta-analysis (Albers, Mantrala & Sridhar, JMR 2010; 506 estimates, 88 data sets, 75 articles): mean current-period sales elasticity to personal selling effort is 0.34 (about 0.31 after method-bias correction), higher for early life-cycle offerings and in Europe, and smaller in recent years. For rough comparison, Sethuraman et al. report a mean short-term ad elasticity of 0.12, but the two meta-analyses differ in construct and method. Use when weighing sales headcount vs media for B2B; estimates are sensitive to model choices.** (2010-09-15; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.47.5.840) `K-4aa9b0f64c`
- **Long-term mix effects (Ataman, van Heerde & Mela, JMR 2010; 70 brands, 25 categories, 5 years, France): total (short plus long-term) sales elasticity was 1.37 for product and 0.74 for distribution, versus 0.13 for advertising and 0.04 for discounting. Discounting's long-term effect was one-third of its short-term effect, while other mix elements had long-term effects over four times their short-term ones. Use to push product and distribution before discounts for mature brands.** (2010-09-15; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.47.5.866) `K-6c7a27ab6a`
- **Choice overload is not a general rule (Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd, JCR 2010): a meta-analysis of 63 conditions from 50 experiments (N=5,036) found a mean effect of virtually zero with large variance across studies, and no sufficient conditions for when more options hurt. Use to test assortment and plan-count changes rather than assuming fewer options always convert better.** (2010-02-10; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1086/651235) `K-96274c13b8`
- **Advertising response is usually convex (Taylor, Kennedy & Sharp, Journal of Advertising Research 2009): single-source data for brands in four consumer goods categories support the prior finding that convex (diminishing-returns) response functions are typical, though not universal. Implication: spreading budget for reach usually beats piling frequency on the same people. Use for media plans favouring reach and continuity; measurement issues and boundary conditions remain open.** (2009-06-01; research, medium) [source](https://doi.org/10.2501/s002184990909028x) `K-c4459a6565`
- **Zero price effect (Shampanier, Mazar & Ariely, Marketing Science 2007): when the price gap between two products is held constant, making the cheaper one free makes dramatically more people choose it and fewer choose the expensive option; people treat free as adding benefit, not just removing cost, with affect the likeliest explanation. Use to design free tiers, free trials and free-shipping offers; experiments with low-priced items, so test on your margins.** (2007-11-01; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.1060.0254) `K-824cc946ee`
- **Update of real-world TV tests (Hu, Lodish & Krieger, JAR 2007; 241 IRI BehaviorScan and matched-market tests, 1989-2003): for established products, increasing TV weight improved sales effectiveness significantly above zero, unlike the 1995 study, driven mainly by more recent tests; average effectiveness rose for tests after 1995. The authors still advise identifying ad effectiveness before launching campaigns. Use to show weight increases can work when the ad itself is effective; US CPG data.** (2007-09-01; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.2501/s0021849907070353) `K-bf29edf8ed`
- **Online reviews and sales (Chevalier & Mayzlin, JMR 2006, Amazon vs Barnes & Noble books): reviews are overwhelmingly positive; an improvement in a book's reviews raised its relative sales on that site; for most samples a one-star review hurt more than a five-star review helped; and review length data suggest buyers read text, not only averages. Use to prioritise responding to and fixing causes of negative reviews. Books, 2000s data.** (2006-07-11; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.43.3.345) `K-e454781042`
- **Price elasticity meta-analysis (Bijmolt, van Heerde & Pieters, JMR 2005): across 1,851 price elasticities from 81 studies, the average is -2.62 (a 1% price cut raises sales about 2.6%). Sales elasticities have grown in magnitude over four decades; models that correct for price endogeneity give larger elasticities, while accounting for heterogeneity does not change them significantly. Use as a prior for price tests; category and brand values vary widely.** (2005-04-13; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.42.2.141.62296) `K-e8c0d4ec27`
- **Price promotions have no permanent effect (Pauwels, Hanssens & Siddarth, JMR 2002, scanner panel, storable and perishable products): permanent promotion effects on category incidence, brand choice and purchase quantity were virtually absent. Over the adjustment period, the long-run decomposition was 66/11/23 (incidence/choice/quantity) for the storable product and 58/39/3 for the perishable one. Use to treat discounts as temporary volume, not lasting share growth.** (2002-11-01; research, medium) [source](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.39.4.421.19114) `K-b6398871b7`
- **Promotions train price sensitivity (Mela, Gupta & Lehmann, JMR 1997, 8.25 years of panel data for a packaged good): consumers became more price- and promotion-sensitive over time, consistent with reduced advertising and increased promotions. Use to warn against habitual discounting: frequent deals can raise long-run price sensitivity and erode pricing power. Single-category evidence, so test before generalizing.** (1997-05-01; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1177/002224379703400205) `K-d9f4bd56ec`
- **Long-term TV effects (Lodish et al., Marketing Science 1995, 55 BehaviorScan weight tests for established packaged goods): when extra TV weight lifted sales significantly in year one, the first year's sales impact roughly doubled over the following two years on average, mainly through a higher buying rate in the test group. When year one showed no significant lift, there was none in the following two years either. Use to judge long-term effects only after a measurable short-term lift; CPG, pre-digital media.** (1995-08-01; research, medium) [source](https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.14.3.g133) `K-dc6e8c8f41`
- **TV split-cable tests (Lodish et al., JMR 1995, 389 BehaviorScan tests): many common beliefs did not separate effective from ineffective TV ads; increasing budgets relative to competitors did not increase sales in general, and standard recall and persuasion copy-test scores were weakly related to sales. Changing brand, copy or media strategy in categories with many purchase occasions and low in-store merchandising raised the odds of an effect. Use to demand in-market tests over copy-test scores.** (1995-05-01; research, high) [source](https://doi.org/10.1177/002224379503200201) `K-bf305cc47f`
